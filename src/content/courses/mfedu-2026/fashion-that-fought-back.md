Advocacy means public support for a cause — and fashion has been one of advocacy's favorite megaphones for over a century, because clothing makes an argument *everywhere its wearer goes*.

## The greatest hits

- **Women's suffrage** — marchers wore white, purple, and green so that any photograph of a crowd became a photograph of the movement. The colors did the counting for them.
- **Zoot suits** — in the 1940s, oversized suits became a defiant symbol of cultural identity for young Mexican-American and Black men, so threatening to the status quo that wearing one could get you attacked. Fabric as resistance.
- **The Black Panthers** — black berets and leather jackets as a uniform of discipline and dignity.
- **The Hong Kong Umbrella Movement** — an everyday object, the yellow umbrella, transformed into an emblem.
- **LGBTQ+ Pride** — the rainbow: six stripes of fabric that became one of the most recognized arguments on Earth.
- **Thailand's Red Shirts and the Guy Fawkes mask** — color and costume as instant, wearable membership.

Notice the pattern: none of these needed a single word. **Color, shape, and repetition carried the entire message.** That's the toolkit you're inheriting.

## Now add technology

Those movements had fabric and dye. You have LEDs, sensors, and microcontrollers. A message that once needed a crowd wearing the same color can now pulse, react, and glow on a single garment rolling down a runway.

## Activity: Decode a movement

Pick one example above (or one you know that we missed). Answer three questions and share with the group:

1. What was the *argument* the clothing made?
2. Why did it work better worn than written?
3. If that movement happened today with light and tech available, what's one thing their garments might do?

> **For teachers:** This pairs well with a quick image search per movement projected on screen. Keep the framing on *how the design worked*, not on debating each cause — the design analysis is the lesson; students bring their own causes next lesson.
