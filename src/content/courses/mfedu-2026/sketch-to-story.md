Time to design *your* piece. Not build — design. The cheapest place to make mistakes is on paper, and good designers make lots of them there on purpose.

## Ideas are the point, drawing isn't

If sketching intimidates you, use a **croquis** — the fashion world's pre-drawn figure. Print the **[Croquis Figures sheet](/courses/mfedu-2026/worksheets/croquis-figures/)**, lay a blank page on top, trace the figure, and design on the tracing. Nobody at MakeFashion Edu has ever been graded on anatomy. Ideas count; rendering doesn't.

Then open the **[Design Notes sheet](/courses/mfedu-2026/worksheets/design-notes/)** and let it be messy: fragments, arrows, doodles, crossed-out versions. Design notes are thinking made visible, not an essay.

## Sketch like our students do

Our designers' finished pieces almost never match their first sketch — and that's the system working. Wings that started as cardboard became fabric over a frame. A "dress with lights" became *Mother Earth* with a glowing crown. The sketch's job is to be a conversation starter between you, your team, and your materials.

Sketch **at least three different versions** before choosing. The first idea is usually the most obvious one — which means it's the one the audience has already seen.

## Two design rules from a hundred runway pieces

1. **Decide where the light lives, on paper, now.** Retrofitting a circuit onto a finished garment is the most common painful surprise in this course. Your sketch should mark battery, lights, and switch like a treasure map.
2. **It sometimes goes backwards, and that's fine.** Some designers get a design idea first and find the story after. Don't force the order — but do finish with both a sketch *and* a story in writing.

## Start your artist statement now

Write one paragraph: *what this piece argues, and why it matters to us.* It's a draft — it will change as the piece does — but teams that write it now build more coherent pieces than teams that write it the night before the show. You'll polish it in the final module.

> **For teachers:** Require the three-version rule; it's where the real ideation happens. The croquis unlocks students who "can't draw" — announce it before anyone has a chance to opt out. Design notes make an excellent artifact for the exhibition display later, so have teams keep everything, including rejects.
