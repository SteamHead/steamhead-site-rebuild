Look down at what you're wearing right now. You made about a dozen decisions this morning — some on purpose, most on autopilot — and every one of them is talking. A team jersey says *I belong to something*. Paint-stained sneakers say *I make things*. A carefully ripped pair of jeans says *these are ripped, but on purpose, and that matters*.

Since the first humans put on the first clothes, outfits have carried meaning: colors once reserved for royalty, stripes forced onto prisoners, uniforms that turn a group of kids into a team. Fashion pulls people together, sets them apart, and — the part this course is about — **makes arguments**. A runway piece is a statement you wear, and by the end of this course, you'll build one that argues for something you believe in, in fabric and light.

## Activity: Read the room

Pair up. Look at what your partner is wearing — really look — and tell them the story you think their outfit is telling today. Then ask them: *"What story do you think I'm telling?"*

Expect surprises. The gap between the story you *meant* to wear and the story someone *read* is exactly the gap designers learn to close.

## One important warning

We do tell stories with our clothes — but nobody's outfit is their *whole* story, and our stories change daily. Reading an outfit is a starting point for curiosity, never a verdict on a person. Stereotyping is the lazy version of what designers do; we're going to do the careful version.

> **For teachers:** Run the pair activity before any vocabulary. If discussion stalls, prompt with professions — what does a chef's uniform argue? A judge's robe? Why does a referee's shirt *need* to be instantly readable? Close on the stereotyping point above; it reliably produces the best discussion of the day.

## Before you move on

- You told someone the story of your own outfit
- You heard a story in someone else's — and got at least one read wrong, which is the point
